\chapter{Performance Engineering and Optimization}
\label{ch:optimization}

The renderer validated in \Cref{ch:validation} is fast by design: at equal quality on the scattering
cloud it reaches the same noise level in less time than the Mitsuba reference (\Cref{ch:results}). The
deliberate engineering behind that result is the subject of this chapter. The work is presented not as a list of speedups but as a
\emph{method}: every candidate is classified by how it can honestly be justified, and only those whose
outcome is genuinely informative are run as controlled ablations. Equally important is what did
\emph{not} work---a ledger of dead ends (\Cref{sec:autopsies}) that together characterise the
performance ceiling of this class of renderer and are themselves a contribution.

\section{The starting gap and the bottleneck}
\label{sec:bottleneck}

NSight Compute locates the bottleneck firmly in
\emph{memory latency}: the kernel is latency-bound on scattered global loads of the per-primitive
\texttt{Primitive} struct, at $21$--$31\%$ occupancy (asset-dependent), itself capped by the code-intrinsic register
pressure of the erf and phase-function chains. The primitive data is already $82$--$98\%$
cache-resident, so reducing its \emph{footprint} cannot help much---the cost is in the latency of
gathering scattered primitives, not in bandwidth. The structural reading of this is the same one that
shaped the architecture (\Cref{sec:gpu-impl}): the per-bounce arithmetic is cheap but the per-ray state
is large and persistent, so the algorithm is \emph{megakernel-shaped}. This single fact predicts
several of the null results in \Cref{sec:autopsies}.

Profiling the production binary quantifies this (NSight Compute, base-clocked). On
the showcase cloud under the measured meadow environment the render megakernel reaches $31.2\%$
occupancy at $45.9\%$ SM and $16.5\%$ DRAM throughput; on the \num{25600}-primitive bunny it is harsher---$20.9\%$ occupancy, the scheduler
with no eligible warp $70\%$ of cycles, DRAM at $1.8\%$---and ray divergence leaves only $5.4$--$6.9$ of
$32$ lanes active per warp. None of the four production assets approaches either roof of the arithmetic
roofline (\Cref{fig:roofline}): all sit at $0.2$--$3\%$ of the RTX~3090's FP32 compute peak and from
$0.6\%$ (tornado) to $16.5\%$ (cloud) of its bandwidth. The regime is unambiguously \emph{latency-bound}
and divergence-dominated---not compute- or bandwidth-bound. Of the two fully profiled, the bunny is the
more extreme because its dense, finely-divided shell maximises traversal divergence while touching almost
no DRAM (the sparse tornado sits lower still on the roofline). The
divergence is exactly what OptiX Shader Execution Reordering~\cite{NvidiaSER2022} targets---regrouping
threads between traversal and shading to restore warp coherence---an Ada-generation feature unavailable on
the Ampere RTX~3090 the development and validation use here. Its effect is therefore measured separately,
directly on an Ada GPU, in \Cref{sec:ser}.

\section{A taxonomy of optimisations}
\label{sec:four-modes}

running only when its outcome is not a foregone conclusion; otherwise the honest justification is a
complexity argument or a citation to standard practice. Each optimisation in this chapter is therefore
placed in one of three modes:

\section{Measured wins}
\label{sec:wins}

The kept speedups---all measured \emph{within this renderer}, each a controlled on/off ablation against
its own validated baseline rather than against the reference---are summarised in
\Cref{tab:wins}. The single largest was eliminating a redundant per-bounce $O(N)$ containment scan;
the most dramatic in relative terms was a smarter shadow-ray transmittance evaluation. Russian-roulette
start depth, a free efficiency knob, was raised from the inherited surface-rendering default of $5$ to
$12$; the controlled sweep (\Cref{fig:rr-depth}) shows \emph{why} this is the right setting while also
revising the magnitude of the win: variance falls and frame time rises monotonically with depth, and
their product is a \emph{shallow basin} spanning depths $8$--$12$ (within $1.2\,\%$ of each other) whose
measured minimum---re-anchored at the calibrated caps on a quiet GPU---lands on the shipped default
$12$, with real penalties outside it ($5$: $+5.0\,\%$; $16$: $+4.9\,\%$ relative to the minimum). The
informal development-time measurement had put the depth-$5\!\to\!12$ gain at ${\sim}11\,\%$; the
controlled \num{16}-seed sweep revises that to $+4.7\,\%$ (depth $5$'s frame-time penalty over the
depth-$12$ minimum). With these wins, and the MIS direct-lighting estimator of \Cref{sec:ris}, in
place, the renderer reaches Mitsuba's noise level in less time on the environment-lit showcase
(\Cref{ch:results}).

\section{The algorithmic highlight: volumetric product-RIS}
\label{sec:ris}

The chapter's headline algorithmic win---a genuine, measured one---is in direct lighting. The baseline next-event estimator spends
one shadow ray per scattering vertex on an environment sample drawn by luminance. Resampled importance
sampling~\cite{Talbot2005} instead draws $K$ environment candidates, resamples one in proportion to a
cheap target (phase value $\times$ environment luminance) via a weighted reservoir~\cite{Bitterli2020},
and traces a shadow ray only for the survivor---paying $K$ cheap evaluations to make the single
expensive shadow ray count. The controlled $16$-seed sweep on the environment-lit showcase measures the
win at $1.48\times$ at equal quality (bootstrap $95\,\%$ CI $[1.47, 1.49]$), on a \emph{plateau}
spanning $K=4$--$6$ (statistically tied) with a genuine turnover beyond: candidate cost rises
monotonically with $K$ while variance falls, and their product peaks where the proposal quality
saturates. The win decomposes into both halves---one shadow ray instead of two ($-21\,\%$ frame time)
\emph{plus} lower variance from the resampling ($-14\,\%$ noise constant); the $K{=}1$ anchor (plain
environment-IS NEE) already wins $1.19\times$ on time alone, \emph{despite} higher variance, which
isolates the resampling's contribution. Crucially the technique is \emph{scene-dependent}, and a three-point peakiness ladder---peakiness being
each environment's peak radiance relative to its mean---makes the
dependence quantitative (\Cref{fig:ris-ksweep}): on a flat (constant) environment, where the candidates
are interchangeable, it is a net loss at every $K$ (best case $1.8\times$ \emph{worse}, degrading to
${\sim}12\times$ at $K{=}1$); on a mid-peak studio environment (peak ${\sim}540\times$ the mean) it already wins
$1.45\times$; and on the high-peak showcase (${\sim}1.5{\times}10^{5}\times$ the mean) $1.48\times$. The win thus rises
with environment peakiness and \emph{saturates} between the studio and showcase points despite a
${\sim}300\times$ difference in peak, on a $K{=}4$--$6$ plateau where the point estimates are
statistically tied. It is therefore
shipped behind a runtime flag (default off, plain MIS; $K=6$ the conservative default), not as an
unconditional change.
The result was validated unbiased against the Mitsuba-analog ground truth on a constant environment
(furnace-clean) \emph{and} on the environment-lit showcase (converged means within the Monte-Carlo
noise floor).

The per-sample variance reduction behind this speedup is visible directly (\Cref{fig:ris-noise}): at
an equal \num{64}-sample budget on the showcase, product-RIS reaches a lower image-space RMSE than
plain MIS \emph{and} costs less per sample, and the two effects compound to the equal-quality
$1.48\times$ above (the RMSE ratio squared, times the per-sample time ratio).

\section{Complexity-argued improvements}
\label{sec:complexity}

The architecture's argmin scatter sampler (\Cref{sec:adt}) improves on the reference asymptotically
rather than by a tuned constant, so it is argued by complexity rather than ablated: there is no on/off
switch to flip---the reference's segment-by-segment marcher is a different algorithm, and re-running it
would only re-confirm that $H$ re-traversals, an ordered $O(H^2)$ boundary march, and a per-segment
root-find cost more than a single unordered argmin over closed-form free flights (\Cref{tab:complexity}).
The supporting $O(1)$ bit-vector active set is justified the same way (\Cref{sec:gpu-impl}); the
active-set inheritance that removes the residual per-bounce $O(N)$ containment scan is, by contrast, a
measured win (\Cref{tab:wins}).

\section{Reasoned decisions, not ablated}
\label{sec:reasoned}

Some choices are deliberately \emph{not} measured. Standard-practice optimisations---multiple CUDA
streams with asynchronous transfers, fused multiply-adds and loop unrolling, \texttt{float4}-coalesced
buffers, the fast-math flag set, GAS compaction and \texttt{PREFER\_FAST\_TRACE}---are cited rather than
ablated, because regressing to ``everything on one stream'' would re-derive a textbook result. One
design decision, by contrast, deserves a direct measurement rather than an argument: analytic versus
tessellated sphere geometry. Porting the historical icosphere mesh into the current renderer behind a
build switch yields a clean A/B in which only the per-primitive geometry differs (\Cref{sec:icosphere}).

\section{Analytic versus tessellated primitive geometry}
\label{sec:icosphere}

The reference~\cite{DSYG} renders each primitive as a \emph{tessellated icosphere} (duplicated per
primitive), reporting a $4.96\times$ speedup over its own custom ray-ellipsoid intersector and a
\num{320}-triangle shell as best. This renderer instead uses OptiX's built-in \emph{analytic} sphere
over a single instanced unit GAS---and also supports the tessellated icosphere behind a compile switch,
so the two geometries can be measured head-to-head with the per-primitive geometry the \emph{only}
difference. The A/B ports the icosphere mesh (subdivision level $\ell$: \num{20}, \num{80}, \num{320},
\num{1280} triangles); the IAS instancing, the any-hit entry collection,
and the analytic optical-depth integration are untouched, with one addition---a convex shell is
entered through a front face and left through a back face, so the any-hit keeps only front-face hits,
preserving the one-entry-per-primitive contract the sphere provides natively.

The analytic sphere was chosen expecting it to be the cheaper path: a single instanced unit-sphere
GAS---one primitive, transformed per instance, rather than a mesh duplicated per primitive---intersected
by OptiX's built-in sphere routine, which we trusted to be efficient. The measurement
(\Cref{tab:icosphere}) refutes that expectation. The tessellated shell is \emph{faster at every
tessellation level}: the analytic sphere pays $1.17$--$1.58\times$ in frame time for its exactness. The
mechanism is the one the reference named: on RTX-class hardware, ray-triangle intersection runs on the
dedicated ray-tracing cores, whereas the built-in sphere is intersected by a \emph{software} routine on
the otherwise hardware-accelerated traversal path. The magnitude
here is smaller than the reference's $4.96\times$ because the baselines differ---their analytic arm
was a custom ellipsoid intersector over per-primitive world-space bounds, where anisotropic primitives
inflate axis-aligned boxes, while this renderer's analytic arm already benefits from the instanced
unit-sphere GAS with per-instance transforms.

Accuracy tells the opposite story, and a non-monotone one. From $\ell{=}0$ to $\ell{=}2$ the error falls
${\sim}4\times$ per subdivision with the physically expected sign (the inscribed shell is slightly
smaller than the true $3\sigma$ ellipsoid, so renders are marginally too bright). At $\ell{=}3$ the
error \emph{rises} on all three assets and flips sign (\Cref{fig:icosphere-sliver}): a localised
population of sliver triangles
mis-places entry points for near-grazing rays, and the count of affected pixels tracks per-primitive
screen footprint rather than anisotropy (the round-primitive cloud is hit hardest). Past $\ell{\approx}2$,
finer tessellation buys worse accuracy at higher cost---consistent with the reference never going
beyond \num{320} triangles.

The decision, then, is a genuine accuracy-versus-performance trade, and this renderer keeps the
analytic sphere \emph{for correctness, at a now-quantified price}. The faceting bias at the best
tessellation ($\ell{=}2$, ${\sim}0.16\,\%$ systematic brightening on the cloud) is invisible in an image
but is an order of magnitude above the $10^{-4}$-level energy agreement that the validation gates of
\Cref{ch:validation} hold the renderer to; the analytic shell is what makes those gates attainable.
Where raw throughput matters more than validation-grade exactness, the measured frontier says
$\ell{=}2$---matching the reference's choice of a \num{320}-triangle icosphere---is the right shell, and
the instanced single-GAS design keeps even that choice memory-trivial (\SI{9.6}{\kilo\byte} compacted,
versus per-primitive duplication in the reference).

\section{Negative results: an autopsy ledger}
\label{sec:autopsies}

The following ideas were implemented or seriously evaluated and did not pay off. Each is reported as
hypothesis, cheap kill-test, measurement, and lesson---collectively a map of where effort on this
renderer does \emph{not} go.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Wavefront refactor (§8.34) --- the big one.} Splitting the megakernel into per-stage
  kernels was, in a development-time measurement, $100$--$1400\times$ \emph{slower} (a wide range across
  configurations; the branch no longer builds on the current toolchain, so this stands as a one-time
  figure): the ${\sim}352$\,B/ray state must stream through DRAM
  every bounce and goes super-linear once the working set exceeds L2. \emph{Lesson:} moving persistent
  per-ray state to global memory is fatal for this workload---it is megakernel-shaped, and this rules
  out a whole family of designs (wavefront, ray-sorting, neural caches).
  \item \textbf{Megakernel footprint reduction (§8.29).} SoA layouts, field reordering, and
  \texttt{\_\_ldg} hints were null: the data is already cache-resident, so the cost is load
  \emph{latency}, not footprint.
  \item \textbf{Cap-free streaming scatter sampling --- the most rigorously killed.} The hit buffer
  exists only to be \emph{re-read} after the argmin; a minimum is an order-independent reduction, so
  in principle the buffer can be deleted: stream the argmin inside the any-hit shader, with the
  free-flight draws made traversal-order-invariant by a stateless counter-based hash of (path, bounce,
  primitive), leaving $O(1)$ per-ray sampling state and no hit-buffer capacity to tune. The
  reformulation was validated to \emph{bit-exactness} against the buffered sampler (furnace plus all
  four assets), rendered the entire lineup with one stock binary and zero overflows---and still lost
  the interleaved A/B against the per-asset-tuned baseline: $+22\,\%$ frame time on the tornado,
  $+16\,\%$ explosion, $+12\,\%$ cloud, $-2\,\%$ bunny. The profile explains why: local-memory
  traffic fell ($-8\,\%$ L2 bytes) but instructions rose ($+6\,\%$) at \emph{unchanged} occupancy
  ---the kernel is register-limited, so local-memory savings cannot buy parallelism, and the extra
  bounded re-traversal plus per-hit any-hit transitions are pure added work on small assets.
  \emph{Lesson:} a cleaner memory shape buys nothing when registers, not local memory, bound
  occupancy. The gated rung ladder that killed it also paid for itself: its bit-exact gate
  root-caused a genuine silent-bias bug in the \emph{buffered} path (a $\chi\!\approx\!0$
  inverse-CDF undershoot that let the winning primitive be excluded from its own active set ---
  since fixed) and established empirically that OptiX does not report hits at exactly
  $t = t_{\max}$; both findings outlived the experiment.
  \item \textbf{Adaptive sampling (§8.30).} Per-pixel convergence stopping was a net loss: at the
  working threshold nothing early-stops on the high-variance cloud, so it matches uniform sampling at
  identical quality but runs \num{0.9}\,\% slower (\num{0.7}\,\% on the calibrated-caps re-confirmation)
and reserves an extra per-pixel variance buffer; a
  more aggressive threshold only trades that overhead for firefly bias. Kept, default-off.
  \item \textbf{Per-step Rao--Blackwellisation, ``A1'' (§8.27) --- the most instructive.} The renderer
  is ${\sim}5\times$ noisier per sample than Mitsuba on the flat-lit scattering cloud
  (\Cref{sec:results-perf}); the hypothesis blamed Mitsuba folding the analytic segment transmittance
  into throughput at \emph{every} bounce versus only at the primary vertex here. A single-Gaussian depth
  sweep (isolating the effect from overlap and from environment sampling --- disabling MIS moved the
  noise by only $1.06\times$) confirmed a real gap but corrected its cause: the two are \emph{different
  estimator classes}. Mitsuba is a \emph{track-length} estimator (transmittance folded into throughput,
  every path a smoothly $T$-weighted escape); the argmin's binary scatter-versus-escape decision
  (\Cref{sec:adt}) makes this a \emph{collision} estimator, which has higher variance for
  transmittance-weighted estimands and a gap that \emph{widens with depth} --- under near-conservative
  flat lighting almost every analog path escapes to the same constant, so Mitsuba self-averages while the
  collision estimator's variance stays flat. The fix is declined on two grounds: the obvious patch
  (adding the continuation escape as a second sampling strategy) replaces the existing \emph{analytic}
  shadow-ray next-event estimator with a higher-variance \emph{stochastic} escape indicator (same
  expectation, strictly worse); and folding transmittance into throughput at every bounce would abandon
  the binary scatter/escape decision that \emph{is} the sort-free argmin. \emph{Lesson:} a characterised
  trade-off, not a missed optimisation --- it helps only the flat regime, while the environment-lit
  showcase (the target) is already won by MIS (\Cref{ch:results}); a hybrid that keeps the argmin yet
  folds the analytic transmittance at all bounces is the open direction should the flat gap ever matter.
  \item \textbf{Density-contribution culling (§8.31)} --- redundant with the BVH's $3\sigma$ bound.
  \textbf{Track-length combine (§8.32)} --- unnecessary, the environment transmittance is already
  analytic. \textbf{Exit-caching (§8.35)} --- bit-identical, no win (the extra local state hit the
  occupancy wall). \textbf{Env-IS alias table (§8.36)} --- correct but sub-$1\%$; environment sampling
  is not the bottleneck. \textbf{Owen--Sobol sampling (§8.20)} --- no win on this high-variance
  integrand.
  \item \textbf{Path guiding (§8.38) --- deferred, not failed.} No \emph{cheap} kill-test could
  validly predict its gain (proxies measure the NEE-handled layer, not the multi-bounce tail), the
  upside is bounded low by the near-diffuse deep field, and the cost is downside-skewed; building it
  would have been a multi-day bet against weak evidence.
\end{itemize}

The unifying lesson is the bottleneck of \Cref{sec:bottleneck}: at low occupancy bound by global-load
latency, footprint and cache micro-optimisations are near-null, and any restructuring that externalises
per-ray state backfires. The one true hardware lever for this divergence---OptiX Shader Execution
Reordering~\cite{NvidiaSER2022}---is Ada-only and therefore unavailable on the Ampere RTX~3090 used here;
\Cref{sec:ser} brings it to bear on an Ada GPU and measures what it recovers.

\section{Shader Execution Reordering: a cross-architecture probe}
\label{sec:ser}

The divergence lever named above---OptiX Shader Execution Reordering (SER)---is unavailable on the
Ampere RTX~3090 this thesis is pinned to, but its effect can be \emph{measured} on Ada-generation
hardware. SER is a hardware feature (Ada, compute capability~8.9, and later) that, at a
programmer-inserted point, repartitions the in-flight threads of a launch into fresh warps grouped by a
coherence key and resumes them: a warp left incoherent by a divergent trace becomes coherent for the
shading and next-bounce work that follows, restoring lane utilisation and coalescing the scattered
\texttt{Primitive} loads that \Cref{sec:bottleneck} identified as the bottleneck. Two properties make it
a clean test of that diagnosis. It is \emph{pure scheduling}---it changes which threads co-execute, not
what each computes, so the image is bit-identical; and it is a lever the reference cannot reach---Mitsuba
renders through Dr.Jit, which compiles one fused megakernel from a high-level trace and emits no reorder
points, whereas this renderer's hand-written argmin megakernel can place a single \texttt{optixReorder}
after the scatter event precisely because it controls its own trace structure.

The renderer was built with SER enabled (one reorder per bounce, keyed on a hash of the quantised scatter
cell) and run against the identical SER-off build on a rented RTX~4090 (Ada), at each asset's calibrated
caps. Because the rented host restricted the profiling counters, the comparison is wall-clock (warm-up
plus eleven interleaved off/on repetitions, \num{64}\,spp, reporting the median; every SER-on image is
bit-identical to its SER-off counterpart---SER reorders \emph{which} threads co-execute, not the
per-thread arithmetic, so each pixel is computed by the same operations either way---and the wall-clock
ratio is therefore also the equal-quality ratio).

SER helps on every asset---\num{1.12} to \num{1.68}$\times$, image-identical (\Cref{tab:ser})---and the
magnitude tracks the amount of divergent \emph{shading} work rather than raw sparsity: it is largest on
the dense, deeply-overlapped bunny ($1.68\times$) and smallest on the thin, mostly-empty tornado
($1.12\times$), whose rays escape quickly and leave little shading to regroup. The coherence
hint is a deliberate design choice, not the default call: the hintless \texttt{optixReorder()} already
helps ($1.25\times$ on the cloud, the hardware grouping by hit alone), but encoding a domain-specific
key---a hash of the scatter cell, so rays that scattered near one another regroup---lifts it to
$1.42\times$. Reordering at \emph{every} bounce, not just the first, is likewise what carries the gain
($1.10\times$ for bounce-0 alone). The win persists under the production direct-lighting estimator
($1.35\times$ with product-RIS). Two things follow. The result \emph{confirms the
\Cref{sec:bottleneck} diagnosis directly}: the one mechanism designed for this exact failure mode---warp
divergence on scattered loads---recovers a third to two-thirds of the frame time. And it exposes an
\emph{architectural} advantage: on SER-capable hardware this renderer pulls further ahead of the
reference precisely because its hand-authored kernel can use a lever the reference's compiled pipeline
cannot. These figures are an Ada cross-architecture probe and do not modify the 3090-based results
reported elsewhere.

\paragraph{From a lever to an end-to-end advantage.}
The architectural gap that SER widens can be measured directly, by re-running the headline equal-quality
comparison (\Cref{sec:results-perf}) on the Ada host against the \emph{same} reference build---Mitsuba
with \texttt{volprim}, the \texttt{volprim\_prb} integrator in its only unbiased (\texttt{use\_nee=false})
analog mode---recompiled there. The estimator's noise constant $k$ is a property of the sampler and the
scene, not the GPU: the Ada measurement reproduced the \num{3090} values exactly
($k_{\text{clip}}=\num{1.887}$ for this renderer, \num{110.6} for the analog reference), so only the
per-sample time differs across architectures and the speedup is fully traceable. The same MIS
configuration that reaches a given noise level $\approx\!59\times$ faster than the analog reference on the
\num{3090} (\Cref{sec:results-perf}) does so $77\times$ faster on the \num{4090}---the hand-written
megakernel scales onto Ada more fully than the reference's Dr.Jit pipeline---and SER lifts this to
$110\times$. With the renderer's best \emph{unbiased} configuration (product-RIS direct lighting,
\Cref{sec:ris}, and the approximate-erf hot path, both inside the validation gates of
\Cref{ch:validation}) the equal-quality advantage reaches $151\times$; additionally enabling the
throughput-optimal icosphere shell (\Cref{sec:icosphere}, which trades a ${\sim}\num{0.1}\%$ systematic
for ray-triangle intersection) reaches $179\times$ (\Cref{tab:ser-eq}). All four are equal-quality
figures---this renderer reaches the reference's noise level in that fraction of the time, most of the
factor coming from the estimator's lower variance rather than from raw per-sample speed. The pinned
headline remains the validation-grade $59\times$ on the \num{3090}; these Ada figures corroborate it and
do not replace it.

\section{Memory}
\label{sec:opt-memory}

Performance is not only time, and the memory story has one result that matters: the megakernel's
per-ray state. The wavefront autopsy above is fundamentally a \emph{memory} story---its ${\sim}352$\,B
of per-ray state, multiplied by the live ray count, is what overruns L2 and collapses performance, the
clearest evidence that the megakernel's register-resident state is not an incidental choice but the
load-bearing one. The acceleration structure itself is not where the budget goes: it is sub-megabyte
for the cloud and a few megabytes for the bunny, built with compaction as a matter of course (a single
build flag, \texttt{OPTIX\_BUILD\_FLAG\_ALLOW\_COMPACTION}, also used by the reference) and dwarfed by
the per-ray state.

\paragraph{Per-asset capacity budgets.} The per-ray buffers are bounded by the worst-case primitive
overlap a scene presents, which the offline estimator of \Cref{sec:gpu-impl} predicts from geometry
alone. \Cref{tab:overlap} reports that estimate across assets. The overlap varies by more than
$25\times$ and---crucially---does not track the primitive count: a \num{4096}-primitive cloud fit
demands an order of magnitude more \emph{active-set} capacity (point overlap \num{529} vs \num{57}) than
a \num{24576}-primitive one, because overlap is set by
how densely the Gaussians pack, not how many there are. No single static cap fits every scene: the
calibrated \num{64}/\num{96} buffers suit the primary cloud, but the denser fits and the bunny would overflow them
and require a larger compile-time cap (which the estimator sizes ahead of the build).
