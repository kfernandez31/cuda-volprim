\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

The validation of \Cref{ch:validation} established that the renderer is \emph{correct}; this chapter
measures what that correctness costs and what it buys. All numbers are taken at the renderer's
operating point---the analytic built-in sphere, multiple-importance-sampled direct lighting, per-asset
calibrated caps, exact \(\operatorname{erf}\)---on the RTX~3090 described in \Cref{sec:setup}, and the
reference throughout is Jorge Condor's Mitsuba \texttt{volprim} implementation of
DSYG~\cite{DSYG} on the same GPU. The chapter reports four axes on which the renderer can be compared to
that reference independently of raw frame time: the energy bias and firefly behaviour of the two
renderers' direct-lighting estimators (\Cref{sec:results-firefly}), the practical noise reduction of
post-process denoising (\Cref{sec:results-denoiser}), the device-memory footprint and what per-asset
cap calibration saves (\Cref{sec:results-memory}), the start-up and iteration latency that matter for
an interactive workflow (\Cref{sec:results-startup}), and a cross-renderer agreement check that
qualifies further assets for comparison (\Cref{sec:results-generalisation}).

\section{Energy bias and fireflies under environment lighting}
\label{sec:results-firefly}

The most consequential cross-renderer difference is not speed but \emph{correctness under production
settings}. Both renderers offer an unbiased analog mode (direct lighting gathered only when a path
escapes to the environment) and a faster mode that estimates direct lighting explicitly at each scatter
vertex---next-event estimation (NEE) in the reference, multiple importance sampling (MIS) here. On a
dense scattering medium these are not interchangeable. \Cref{fig:g1-bias} renders the showcase cloud
under the measured meadow environment three ways, each the mean of \num{16} independent \num{64}-spp
seeds so that the differences are systematic, not noise: this renderer's MIS estimator, the reference's
NEE estimator, and the reference's analog mode as an unbiased ground truth.

\begin{figure}[htp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/g1_bias.pdf}
  \caption{Direct-lighting estimators on the showcase cloud (meadow environment, mean of \num{16}
  \num{64}-spp seeds). \emph{Left:} this renderer's MIS estimator, \num{0.4}\,\% above the unbiased
  ground truth---correct to within Monte-Carlo noise. \emph{Centre:} the reference's NEE estimator,
  \num{156}\,\% too bright---a gross energy bias that washes the cloud out. \emph{Right:} the
  reference's analog mode, the unbiased ground truth (and visibly the noisiest of the three).}
  \label{fig:g1-bias}
\end{figure}

Anchored to the analog ground truth (mean radiance \num{0.3201}), this renderer's MIS result is
\num{0.3214}---a \num{+0.4}\,\% difference, inside the Monte-Carlo noise floor---while the reference's
NEE result is \num{0.8199}, \emph{\num{+156}\,\% too bright}. The reference's explicit direct-lighting
estimator does not converge to the correct image on this medium; its only unbiased mode is therefore
the analog one. That matters for any equal-quality comparison, because the analog mode is also by far
the noisiest: at equal render time the reference-analog image carries roughly \(59\times\) the
clipped per-pixel variance of this renderer's MIS image (the clipped statistic, at the
\num{99.9}th percentile, discounts the rare extreme fireflies that dominate the raw variance by a
further order of magnitude). The renderer thus reaches a given noise level on the showcase
substantially faster than the reference's only unbiased configuration---\emph{and} it does so without
the firefly tail the analog mode exhibits. This is not a handicap imposed on the reference: its faster
estimator is simply biased here (\Cref{fig:g1-bias}, centre), so the honest unbiased baseline is the
firefly-prone analog one.

\section{Denoising}
\label{sec:results-denoiser}

Where a converged image is not required, the integrated OptiX denoiser (a runtime flag,
\Cref{sec:gpu-impl}; not part of the unbiased pipeline) is an effective post-process.
\Cref{fig:denoise} denoises a \num{16}-spp cloud render against a \num{1024}-spp reference: the
image-space RMSE falls from \num{0.353} to \num{0.049}, a \(7.2\times\) reduction, removing the
fireflies and most of the stochastic grain while preserving the silhouette and the large-scale shading.
As a learned post-process the denoiser trades a controlled, content-dependent bias for this noise
reduction---it is reported as a practical option, not as part of the validated estimator---and its
equal-quality effective-sample gain on the showcase is recorded in \Cref{tab:wins}.

\begin{figure}[htp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/denoise.pdf}
  \caption{The OptiX denoiser on a \num{16}-spp cloud render (meadow environment) against a
  \num{1024}-spp reference. RMSE versus the reference falls \(7.2\times\) (\num{0.353} to \num{0.049});
  the denoiser is a post-process and is not part of the unbiased pipeline.}
  \label{fig:denoise}
\end{figure}

\section{Memory footprint}
\label{sec:results-memory}

The renderer's device-memory footprint is dominated not by scene geometry but by the per-ray state
reserved for the path-tracing kernel. The hit buffer and active set (\Cref{sec:gpu-impl}) live in
thread-local memory, which the driver reserves for the GPU's maximum resident thread count; their
compile-time capacities therefore set a scene-independent floor on device memory, while the
acceleration structure itself is comparatively negligible (the instanced unit-sphere GAS is
\SI{0.10}{\mega\byte} compacted for the cloud, \SI{3.97}{\mega\byte} for the \num{25600}-Gaussian
bunny). \Cref{tab:vram} measures the peak per-process device memory for each asset, comparing the
per-asset calibrated build against the universal ``safe'' \(512/512\) build that a user without the
calibration tooling would pick.

\begin{table}[htp]
  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    Asset & caps (active/hit) & calibrated & safe \(512/512\) & saved \\
    \midrule
    cloud     & \(64/96\)   & 578\,MiB & 1200\,MiB & 622\,MiB (52\,\%) \\
    tornado   & \(112/384\) & 818\,MiB & 1200\,MiB & 382\,MiB (32\,\%) \\
    explosion & \(32/160\)  & 600\,MiB & 1200\,MiB & 600\,MiB (50\,\%) \\
    bunny     & \(80/528\)  & 900\,MiB & 1200\,MiB & 300\,MiB (25\,\%) \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Peak device memory (per-process, polled during a render). The safe build reserves a flat
  1200\,MiB on every asset---confirming the per-ray reservation, not the geometry, dominates.
  Per-asset calibration cuts this to each asset's true demand, saving $0.30$--$0.62\,$GiB
  ($25$--$52\,\%$); the saving tracks the hit-buffer cap (the near-\(512\) bunny saves
  least).}
  \label{tab:vram}
\end{table}

The saving is large enough to change the cross-renderer comparison. On the cloud, the calibrated build
peaks at 578\,MiB---\emph{below} the reference's 838\,MiB on the same scene---whereas the
untuned safe build, at 1200\,MiB, exceeds it by \num{43}\,\%. Per-asset cap calibration is thus
what places this renderer below the reference on memory as well as keeping it below it on the frame time
the same caps govern (\Cref{sec:gpu-impl}); without the tooling, the conservative sizing would forfeit
both advantages.

\section{Startup and iteration latency}
\label{sec:results-startup}

For an interactive or iterative workflow the relevant cost is not only the converged frame time but the
fixed latency before the first pixel. The reference, built on Mitsuba's just-in-time-compiled Dr.Jit
backend, pays a one-time kernel-compilation cost on each fresh process: \SI{0.78}{\second} warm (cached
kernels) and \SI{2.20}{\second} cold. This renderer's ahead-of-time-compiled OptiX-IR pipeline loads
and launches in \SI{0.39}{\second}---roughly half the reference's warm start and an order of magnitude
under its cold start. The advantage is structural (compilation happens at build time, not run time) and
compounds across the many short renders a parameter sweep or a debugging session entails.

\section{Cross-renderer generalisation}
\label{sec:results-generalisation}

Beyond the cloud, the renderer was checked against the reference on two further DSYG assets---a tornado
and an explosion plume---using Jorge's native primitive files so that both renderers consume identical
geometry. Comparing the converged-mean radiance (a flip-invariant energy ratio, \Cref{sec:money-shot})
gives \num{0.99911} on the tornado and \num{1.00006} on the explosion: cloud-class agreement, to within
\num{0.01}--\num{0.09}\,\%. These gates confirm that the renderer's correctness is not cloud-specific
and qualify both assets for the equal-quality timing comparison.
