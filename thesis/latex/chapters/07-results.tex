\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

The validation of \Cref{ch:validation} established that the renderer is \emph{correct}; this chapter
measures what that correctness costs and what it buys. All numbers are taken at the renderer's
operating point---the analytic built-in sphere, multiple-importance-sampled direct lighting, per-asset
calibrated caps, exact \(\operatorname{erf}\)---on the RTX~3090 described in \Cref{sec:setup}, and the
reference throughout is Condor's Mitsuba \texttt{volprim} implementation of
DSYG~\cite{DSYG} on the same GPU. Beyond the headline equal-quality result (\Cref{sec:results-perf}),
the chapter reports five axes on which the renderer can be compared to that reference independently of raw frame time: the energy bias and firefly behaviour of the two
renderers' direct-lighting estimators (\Cref{sec:results-firefly}), the practical noise reduction of
post-process denoising (\Cref{sec:results-denoiser}), the device-memory footprint and what per-asset
cap calibration saves (\Cref{sec:results-memory}), the start-up and iteration latency that matter for
an interactive workflow (\Cref{sec:results-startup}), and a cross-renderer agreement check that
qualifies further assets for comparison (\Cref{sec:results-generalisation}). A final section measures how
render time and device memory scale with primitive count (\Cref{sec:results-scaling}).

\section{Equal-quality performance versus Mitsuba}
\label{sec:results-perf}

The headline result is an equal-quality comparison on the environment-lit showcase. Because the
reference's fast direct-lighting estimator (NEE) is energy-biased on this medium
(\Cref{sec:results-firefly}), its \emph{only} unbiased configuration is the high-variance analog one.
Against that honest baseline, this renderer---whose MIS direct lighting is both correct and
low-variance---reaches a given noise level roughly \(\mathbf{59\times}\) faster at equal render
time.\footnote{Equal-quality basis throughout this chapter (as introduced in \Cref{ch:validation}):
the noise constant $k=\bar v\cdot\mathrm{spp}$, with $\bar v$ the mean per-pixel variance across
independent seeds; the equal-quality speedup is the ratio of $k\cdot t$ (render time $t$). The
\num{59} uses \emph{clipped} $k$---radiance clipped at the \num{99.9}th percentile of the whole image
set (a single global threshold) before $\bar v$, which discounts the sparse fireflies; the raw,
firefly-dominated ratio is far larger ($\approx\!2000\times$).} This
is the production-mode figure: the configuration one would actually use.

\paragraph{Isolating the sampler.} To separate the \emph{sampler} from the direct-lighting estimator,
the two renderers can be compared in the \emph{same} (analog) mode---no NEE on either side. Under the
meadow environment this comparison is metric-unstable: this renderer's analog variance is
firefly-dominated (rare bright-sun escapes), so raw per-pixel variance favours the reference while
firefly-clamped variance favours this renderer, and no single equal-quality figure survives. A
\emph{flat} (uniform) environment removes the fireflies on both sides and makes the comparison stable.
There, at an equal sample count, this renderer is \(\mathbf{\sim\!3\times}\) faster per sample
(\SI{2.90}{\second} versus \(\sim\)\SI{8.5}{\second} at \num{64}\,spp on the cloud)\footnote{The
flat-env Mitsuba-analog frame time was not separately recorded; \SI{8.5}{\second} is the
meadow-analog steady state reused, which the flat-env case can only match or beat, so the
\(\sim\!3\times\) is if anything conservative. The \SI{2.90}{\second} is our locked-clock
median.}---the single-trace argmin needs no per-segment root-find, segment march, or shadow connection---but carries \(\sim\!5\times\)
the per-sample variance (both estimators unbiased; the converged means agree to \num{0.02}\,\%; the gap
is the collision- versus track-length-estimator trade-off dissected in \Cref{sec:autopsies}), so the
\emph{net} equal-quality figure is \(\sim\!0.6\times\). The architectural contribution
(\Cref{ch:architecture}) is therefore a \emph{structural, throughput} simplification---march-free,
sort-free, root-find-free, faster per sample---\emph{not} a per-sample variance reduction. What actually wins at
equal quality is the MIS direct-lighting estimator---low-variance under the peaky environments that
matter (\Cref{sec:ris})---not the argmin sampler on its own.

\paragraph{The headline is environment importance sampling.} That \(59\times\) is specific to peaky
illumination, and honestly so. On the same flat environment the MIS estimator carries no advantage at
all: its per-vertex next-event estimation is wasted work when every direction returns the same radiance,
leaving this renderer roughly an order of magnitude \emph{noisier} than the reference's analog mode at
equal time. The \(59\times\) is thus the payoff of importance-sampling a peaky HDR environment---which
our MIS does and the reference's only unbiased mode (analog) does not---rather than a generic sampler
win. This mirrors the resampled direct-lighting result of \Cref{sec:ris}, where the very same
environment-sampling machinery helps under peaky illumination and is counterproductive on flat: the
renderer's advantage is concentrated exactly where production volumetric lighting lives.

\paragraph{Beyond the cloud.} The bunny (\num{25600} Gaussians) renders in \SI{50.4}{\second} at
\num{64}\,spp, \(512^2\), under the meadow environment (the white-constant scattering configuration of
\Cref{tab:asset-cost} is \SI{53.9}{\second}; same order), with zero cap overflows---an internal scaling
point (its three native fit variants make a matched cross-renderer comparison ambiguous, so it is not
run against Mitsuba for the \emph{quality} comparison of \Cref{sec:results-generalisation}; its memory
is still compared in \Cref{tab:vram}, where the fit choice is immaterial).

\section{Energy bias and fireflies under environment lighting}
\label{sec:results-firefly}

The most consequential cross-renderer difference is not speed but \emph{correctness under production
settings}. Both renderers offer an unbiased analog mode (direct lighting gathered only when a path
escapes to the environment) and a faster mode that estimates direct lighting explicitly at each scatter
vertex: next-event estimation (NEE) in the reference, and here that same next-event estimation combined
with phase-function sampling under multiple importance sampling (MIS)---so this renderer also performs
NEE, but MIS-weighted with phase sampling rather than alone. On a
dense scattering medium these are not interchangeable. \Cref{fig:g1-bias} renders the showcase cloud
under the measured meadow environment three ways, each the mean of \num{16} independent \num{64}-spp
seeds so that the differences are systematic, not noise: this renderer's MIS estimator, the reference's
NEE estimator, and the reference's analog mode as an unbiased ground truth.

\begin{figure}[htp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/g1_bias.pdf}
  \caption{Direct-lighting estimators on the showcase cloud (meadow environment, mean of \num{16}
  \num{64}-spp seeds). \emph{Left:} this renderer's MIS estimator, \num{0.4}\,\% above the unbiased
  ground truth---correct to within Monte-Carlo noise. \emph{Centre:} the reference's NEE estimator,
  \num{156}\,\% too bright---a gross energy bias that washes the cloud out. \emph{Right:} the
  reference's analog mode, the unbiased ground truth (and visibly the noisiest of the three). All three
  use the \emph{identical} scene and sample budget (\num{16}\,$\times$\,\num{64}\,spp), so the
  comparison is fair: the brightness differences are estimator \emph{bias} and the noise differences are
  estimator \emph{variance}---both intrinsic to the estimators, not artefacts of the setup. Our MIS
  estimator is the only one that is simultaneously correct and clean.}
  \label{fig:g1-bias}
\end{figure}

Anchored to the analog ground truth (mean radiance \num{0.3201}), this renderer's MIS result is
\num{0.3214}---a \num{+0.4}\,\% difference, inside the Monte-Carlo noise floor---while the reference's
NEE result is \num{0.8199}, \emph{\num{+156}\,\% too bright}. The reference's explicit direct-lighting
estimator does not converge to the correct image on this medium; its only unbiased mode is therefore
the analog one. That matters for any equal-quality comparison, because the analog mode is also by far
the noisiest: at equal render time the reference-analog image carries roughly \(59\times\) the
clipped per-pixel variance of this renderer's MIS image (the clipped statistic, at the
\num{99.9}th percentile, discounts the rare extreme fireflies that dominate the raw variance by a
further order of magnitude). The renderer thus reaches a given noise level on the showcase
substantially faster than the reference's only unbiased configuration---\emph{and} it does so without
the firefly tail the analog mode exhibits. This is not a handicap imposed on the reference: its faster
estimator is simply biased here (\Cref{fig:g1-bias}, centre), so the honest unbiased baseline is the
firefly-prone analog one.

\section{Denoising}
\label{sec:results-denoiser}

The optimisations so far are the renderer's own. NVIDIA's OptiX denoiser is a bonus of a different
kind: a single library call wired in behind a runtime flag (\Cref{sec:gpu-impl}) at essentially no
engineering cost, sitting entirely outside the unbiased estimator---but a large practical win wherever a
converged image is not required. \Cref{fig:denoise} denoises a \num{16}-spp cloud render against a
\num{1024}-spp reference: the image-space RMSE falls from \num{0.353} to \num{0.049}, a \(7.2\times\)
reduction, removing the fireflies and most of the stochastic grain while preserving the silhouette and
the large-scale shading---an effective ${\sim}30\times$ sample-equivalent on the showcase. As a learned
post-process it trades a controlled, content-dependent bias for this noise reduction, so it is reported
as a practical accelerator for beauty renders, not as part of the validated estimator.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/denoise.pdf}

  \num{1024}-spp reference. RMSE versus the reference falls \(7.2\times\) (\num{0.353} to \num{0.049});
  the denoiser is a post-process and is not part of the unbiased pipeline.}

\section{Memory footprint}
\label{sec:results-memory}

The renderer's device-memory footprint is dominated not by scene geometry but by the per-ray state
reserved for the path-tracing kernel. The hit buffer and active set (\Cref{sec:gpu-impl}) live in
thread-local memory, which the driver reserves for the GPU's maximum resident thread count; their
compile-time capacities therefore set a scene-independent floor on device memory, while the
acceleration structure itself is comparatively negligible (the instanced unit-sphere GAS is
\SI{0.10}{\mega\byte} compacted for the cloud, \SI{3.97}{\mega\byte} for the \num{25600}-Gaussian
bunny). The comparison that matters is against the reference: on the cloud, the renderer's peak device
memory is \textbf{\SI{578}{MiB}---\num{31}\,\% below Mitsuba's \SI{838}{MiB}} on the identical scene.
Across the suite the picture is mixed, and worth stating plainly: the calibrated build sits below Mitsuba
on the cloud and explosion (\num{31}\,\% and \num{26}\,\% less) but \emph{above} it on the denser tornado
and bunny (\SI{818}{MiB} vs \SI{806}{MiB}, and \SI{900}{MiB} vs \SI{806}{MiB}), whose heavy overlap and
\num{25600} primitives force the largest per-ray hit-buffer reservations. The memory win is therefore real
where the worst-case per-ray hit count is modest and turns into a small premium on the densest assets---a
direct consequence of the per-ray-reservation model (\Cref{sec:gpu-impl}), not of scene geometry.
\Cref{tab:vram} reports the per-asset calibrated peak and, for context, what the calibration buys over a
conservative \(512/512\) fallback (the smallest cap that is guaranteed not to overflow without per-asset
measurement)---the difference between sitting below the reference on memory and above it.

Calibration is what secures that win: the untuned \(512/512\) fallback would reserve 1200\,MiB on the
cloud---\emph{exceeding} Mitsuba's 838\,MiB by \num{43}\,\%---so without per-asset measurement the
renderer would sit above the reference on memory rather than below it, on the same caps that also govern
its frame time (\Cref{sec:gpu-impl}). The per-asset savings range from \num{25} to \num{52}\,\%
(\Cref{tab:vram}).

\section{Startup and iteration latency}
\label{sec:results-startup}

For an interactive or iterative workflow the relevant cost is not only the converged frame time but the
fixed latency before the first pixel. The timings below are each the time-to-first-render on the cloud scene
(median of three fresh processes, isolated as first-render minus steady-state frame time). The
reference, built on Mitsuba's just-in-time-compiled Dr.Jit
backend, pays a one-time kernel-compilation cost on each fresh process: \SI{0.78}{\second} warm (cached
kernels) and \SI{2.20}{\second} cold. This renderer's ahead-of-time-compiled OptiX-IR pipeline loads
and launches in \SI{0.39}{\second}---roughly half the reference's warm start and about a fifth of its
cold start. The advantage is structural (compilation happens at build time, not run time),
asset-independent (it is pipeline setup, not scene work), and
compounds across the many short renders a parameter sweep or a debugging session entails.

\section{Cross-renderer generalisation}
\label{sec:results-generalisation}

Beyond the cloud, the renderer was checked against the reference on two further DSYG assets---a tornado
and an explosion plume---using Condor's native primitive files so that both renderers consume identical
geometry. Each is rendered in uniform-albedo-\num{0.9} scattering under a constant-white environment from a matched diagonal
view (the configuration that makes the comparison reference-adjudicable, \Cref{sec:money-shot}), and the
metric is the converged-mean radiance ratio.
The ratio is \num{0.99911} on the tornado and \num{1.00006} on the explosion: cloud-class agreement, to
within \num{0.006}--\num{0.09}\,\%. \Cref{fig:generalisation} shows that the agreement is not merely a
mean coincidence: the two renderers' images are visually indistinguishable, and their amplified
difference is unstructured---it has no localised feature, which by the differential-validation criterion
of \Cref{sec:reference-problem} (a \emph{structured} difference localises a discrepancy; an unstructured,
mean-matching one is consistent with Monte-Carlo noise) is the signature of agreement rather than a
residual bias---bounding any systematic difference below the precision the test resolves, rather than
proving it exactly zero. These
gates confirm that the renderer's correctness is not cloud-specific and qualify both assets for the
equal-quality timing comparison.

  \centering

  \caption{Cross-renderer generalisation on two further DSYG assets, uniform-albedo-\num{0.9} scattering under a constant-white
  environment from a matched diagonal view, \num{256}\,spp. \emph{Left to right:} this renderer,
  Mitsuba-analog, and the absolute difference amplified \(10\times\). The converged-mean ratios are

\section{Scaling with primitive count}
\label{sec:results-scaling}

A renderer organised around hardware ray tracing should pay for primitives the way the acceleration
structure does---sub-linearly, because each ray integrates only the primitives it actually enters, not
the whole scene. Measuring this cleanly requires isolating the primitive count from everything that
co-varies with it in a real asset---packing density, spatial extent, optical thickness---so the scaling
claim rests on a \emph{controlled synthetic sweep} (\Cref{fig:scaling}), with the production assets
reported separately as operating costs (\Cref{tab:asset-cost}) rather than as a scaling series, since
across real assets \(N\) is confounded with density (\Cref{tab:overlap}). All timings are medians of
three seeds at the pinned clocks of \Cref{sec:setup} (\num{512}\textsuperscript{2}, \num{64}\,spp).

\begin{figure}[htp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/scaling.pdf}
  \caption{Render time (\emph{left}) and device memory (\emph{right}) versus primitive count \(N\),
  log--log. \emph{Left:} a controlled synthetic sweep of \(k\times k\) grids of \emph{identical}
  Gaussians (one fixed binary, constant caps). The fit \(t\propto N^{0.40}\) (\(R^2=0.96\)) is over the
  four \emph{square} grids \(N\in\{16,256,1024,4096\}\) (filled), which hold a constant \(1\!:\!1\)
  aspect so only the count changes---a \(256\times\) increase in primitives costs only
  \(\sim\!10\times\) the time, the sub-linear behaviour the BVH affords. The three \(1\!:\!2\)
  rectangular grids up to \(N=8192\) (open markers) change aspect and screen coverage, confounding
  \(N\), and are excluded from the fit. Production assets are not plotted here (they confound \(N\)
  with packing density; their costs are in \Cref{tab:asset-cost}).
  \emph{Right:} peak device memory is \emph{flat} in \(N\) at a fixed
  cap (the synthetic sweep holds the \(512/512\) safe build at \SI{1200}{MiB} from \(N=16\) to
  \num{8192}), because the per-ray local-memory reservation dominates (\Cref{sec:results-memory}); the
  only \(N\)-dependent term, the compacted BVH, grows linearly at \(\sim\!\SI{0.16}{KB}\) per primitive
  and stays two-to-three orders of magnitude below the reservation (\SI{0.10}{MB} at \num{652}
  primitives to \SI{3.97}{MB} at \num{25600}).}
  \label{fig:scaling}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Time.} The synthetic sweep measured seven grids; the \emph{square} family alone---identical
Gaussians, identical caps, constant \(1\!:\!1\) aspect, only the count changing
(\(N\in\{16,256,1024,4096\}\))---isolates \(N\) cleanly, because the three \(1\!:\!2\) rectangular grids
change aspect and screen coverage and so confound the count (the full series is non-monotonic). Over the
four square grids render time fits a \emph{power law} \(t\propto N^{0.40}\)---a straight line in the
log--log plot, \(\log t = b\log N + \mathrm{const}\) fitted by least squares, with exponent \(b=0.40\)
(\(n=4\), \(R^2=0.96\)): the fitted exponent implies a per-doubling factor of \(\sim\!1.3\times\) rather
than \(2\times\), and a \(256\times\)
increase in primitives costs only \(\sim\!10\times\) the time. This is the acceleration structure doing its job: per-ray work is bounded
by the primitives a ray actually traverses, not the scene total, so the geometry cost is firmly
sub-linear in \(N\). \Cref{tab:asset-cost} lists the four production assets' costs under a matched
configuration. They are deliberately \emph{not} read as a scaling curve: from the cloud (\num{652}) to
the bunny (\num{25600}) is a \num{39}\(\times\) jump in primitives for a \num{15}\(\times\) increase in
time, but the bunny is also a denser, more occluding medium, so part of that growth is deeper paths, not
traversal---and the per-asset overlap that drives both cost and capacity does not track \(N\) at all
(\Cref{tab:overlap}). The honest scaling statement is therefore the controlled one: \emph{geometry} cost
is sub-linear in primitive count; the residual asset-to-asset variation is the physics of the medium.

\paragraph{Memory.} Device memory is effectively decoupled from \(N\). The dominant term, the per-ray
state reserved for the kernel, is fixed by the compile-time caps and is scene-size independent: the
safe \(512/512\) build holds a flat \SI{1200}{MiB} from \num{16} to \num{8192} primitives
(\Cref{fig:scaling}, right). The only term that grows with \(N\)---the acceleration structure---is
linear and negligible, \(\sim\!\SI{0.16}{KB}\) per primitive, ranging from \SI{0.10}{MB} on the cloud to
\SI{3.97}{MB} on the bunny: two to three orders of magnitude under the reservation. Because the caps
themselves track overlap density rather than count (\Cref{tab:overlap}), neither term ties device memory
to the primitive count---a calibrated build's footprint is governed by how densely the medium packs, not
how many Gaussians it contains.
