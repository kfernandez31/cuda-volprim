\chapter{Validation Methodology and Correctness}
\label{ch:validation}

A renderer that introduces a new sampling scheme (\Cref{ch:architecture}) must earn trust: the
sort-free analog-decomposition sampler is only useful if it produces the \emph{same} images a correct
volumetric path tracer would, and the production optimisations of \Cref{ch:optimization} are only
admissible if they do not perturb that result. This chapter establishes that correctness. It first
explains \emph{how} the renderer is validated---a differential methodology forced by the absence of an
absolute ground truth (\Cref{sec:reference-problem})---and the statistics needed to compare two
stochastic renderers fairly (\Cref{sec:compare-correctly}). It then walks a ladder of increasingly
complex scenes, from a single Gaussian with a closed-form answer up to the full cloud, in absorption
(\Cref{sec:absorption-ladder}) and then in scattering (\Cref{sec:scattering-ladder}), validates each
rendering feature against the reference (\Cref{sec:feature-validation}), and closes on the combined
showcase (\Cref{sec:money-shot}). Throughout, the ladder doubles as the empirical
\emph{unbiasedness proof} of the analog-decomposition sampler: were the argmin rule biased, the
discrepancy would appear first on the scenes with a known analytic answer.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

All results in this thesis were measured on a single workstation: an NVIDIA GeForce RTX~3090 (Ampere,
compute capability~8.6, \SI{24}{\giga\byte}), with the renderer built on CUDA and OptiX~9 in its
release configuration (\texttt{-O3}, \texttt{-{}-use\_fast\_math}). The Mitsuba~3 reference runs on the
same GPU through its CUDA backend, so cross-renderer comparisons are on identical hardware. Unless noted
otherwise, every timing figure is taken at a single fixed operating point---the card's sustained full
clock---to avoid clock-throttling artefacts (\Cref{sec:compare-correctly}); the equal-quality metric,
being a ratio, is robust to the operating point regardless.

\section{Differential validation}
\label{sec:reference-problem}

No absolute, per-asset ground truth is available for these Gaussian assets, so correctness is
established \emph{differentially}---by quantitative comparison against a trusted reference renderer,
backed by analytic ground truth wherever a scene admits a closed form.

The renderer is compared against Mitsuba~3~\cite{Mitsuba3}---configured identically,
primitive-for-primitive, from the same Gaussian data---and the difference is quantified. Mitsuba is
mature, widely trusted, and runs the same physically based volumetric path tracing, so a small,
converging difference is strong evidence of correctness, while a structured difference localises a
bug. This turns an unfalsifiable ``does it look right?'' into a measurable gap, and it is the backbone
of every result below. Where an even stronger check exists---a scene with a closed-form answer---the
renderer is held to that analytic ground truth directly.

\section{How to compare correctly}
\label{sec:compare-correctly}

Comparing two Monte~Carlo renderers is itself error-prone, and several pitfalls had to be controlled.
\emph{Bias and variance must be separated.} A single per-pixel RMSE between two noisy renders conflates
estimator \emph{bias} (a systematic difference that survives convergence) with \emph{variance} (noise
that vanishes as samples grow). Concretely, for two independent renders $A$ and $B$ of the same scene,
the expected per-pixel squared difference splits cleanly into the two,
\[
  \mathbb{E}\big[(A-B)^2\big]
  = \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[A]-\mathbb{E}[B]\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}
  + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}[A]+\operatorname{Var}[B]}_{\text{noise}},
\]
so a single raw difference cannot, on its own, tell a genuine discrepancy from sampling noise.
Re-rendering each image under several independent seeds estimates the noise term directly (as the
inter-seed variance); when the measured difference is statistically consistent with that noise floor,
the bias term is zero to within the noise---the precise sense in which two renders are said here to
``match to within Monte~Carlo noise.'' The renderer is therefore checked for bias by comparing
\emph{converged} means---or, where available, against an analytic value---while \emph{efficiency} is
compared separately and at \emph{equal quality}: since Monte~Carlo error falls as
$O(1/\sqrt{N})$ (\Cref{sec:monte-carlo}), the meaningful quantity is the noise constant
$k = \mathrm{RMSE}^2 \cdot N$, and one renderer is faster than another only if it reaches the same $k$
in less time. Raw frame-time comparisons at fixed sample count are reported only alongside this
equal-quality figure (\Cref{ch:results}).

Two further controls matter. \emph{Fireflies}---rare, very bright samples---inflate RMSE out of
proportion to perceived error, so robust statistics (high percentiles, clipped means) accompany the raw
numbers, and the difference in firefly behaviour between the renderers is itself a result
(\Cref{ch:results}). And all timing is reported at a single, fixed GPU operating point, because clock
throttling otherwise injects spurious variation; the equal-quality metric, being a ratio, is robust to
it regardless.

\section{Absorption: the correctness ladder}
\label{sec:absorption-ladder}

With scattering disabled (albedo $0$), the image depends only on transmittance, which for a Gaussian
mixture is the closed-form optical depth of \Cref{sec:analytic-od}---so the ground truth is
\emph{analytic}, independent of any reference renderer. This isolates the geometry and the optical-depth
integration from the stochastic scattering logic.

\paragraph{Single Gaussian.} A single primitive integrates to a closed-form transmittance at every
pixel. The renderer matches this analytic value to within floating-point tolerance, confirming the
ray--primitive transform, the erf-based optical depth, and the camera model. The small residual that
remains is pure pixel-filter variance, not bias.

\paragraph{Overlap ladder.} Stacking primitives tests the additivity of optical depth and the
active-set bookkeeping that the argmin sampler depends on. Up to the active-set capacity the renderer
matches the analytic transmittance of the combined medium to $\sim\!10^{-4}$. A deliberately degenerate
\emph{collinear} stress test---$K$ identical Gaussians stacked so the exact answer is a single
$K$-fold-denser Gaussian---exposed, and then confirmed the fix for, the cap-overflow behaviour discussed
in \Cref{sec:gpu-impl}: past the old capacity the active set silently dropped primitives, producing a
predictable under-absorption that matched $\exp(-\min(\text{cap},K)/K \cdot \tau_0)$ to $10^{-4}$. The
same stress test surfaced a \emph{reference} limitation---Mitsuba's volumetric primitive integrator
itself under-absorbs as identical primitives are stacked---a reminder that "differential" cuts both
ways and that the analytic check is the final arbiter.

\paragraph{The cloud.} The full $652$-primitive cloud, validated against the analytic absorption ground
truth across all camera views, matches view-independently. This established absorption correctness
at scene scale before scattering---and hence multiple sampled bounces---was introduced.
\Cref{fig:absorption-ladder} collects the ladder.

\section{Scattering validation}
\label{sec:scattering-ladder}

Enabling scattering (albedo $>0$) activates the full path tracer: the analog-decomposition sampler, the
phase function, and next-event estimation over many bounces. There is no closed-form image here, so two
checks are used: a reference-free energy invariant, and the differential comparison against Mitsuba.

\paragraph{Furnace test.} In a uniform environment with albedo $1$ and no absorption, energy
conservation demands that every pixel return exactly the background---any deviation is estimator bias.
The renderer passes this furnace test flat to within noise, confirming the scattering loop conserves
energy. Notably, Mitsuba's \emph{next-event} integrator fails the same test by $\approx\!6.5\%$
(an over-estimate from its direct-lighting term), so its \emph{analog} (NEE-disabled) mode---which is
unbiased---is used as the scattering ground truth throughout.

\paragraph{The scattering ladder.} Against Mitsuba-analog the renderer is validated up the same ladder:
a single scattering Gaussian, clusters, and the full cloud, each at albedo $0.9$. Single and cluster
rungs match within noise; the cloud rung passes view-independently. These passes are the empirical
evidence that the argmin scatter sampler (\Cref{sec:adt}) is unbiased: a wrong free-flight distribution
would bias the converged mean away from the independently-correct Mitsuba-analog result, and it does
not (\Cref{fig:scattering-ladder}).

\section{Feature validation}
\label{sec:feature-validation}

Each rendering feature is gated against the reference before it is trusted in combination. Several
genuine bugs were found and fixed this way---evidence that the differential method has teeth.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Environment-map orientation.} A constant or isotropic environment cannot reveal an
  orientation error; only a full-sphere directional test can. Such a test exposed a vertical-flip bug in
  the HDR loader, which was fixed and then confirmed against Mitsuba across viewing directions.
  \item \textbf{Real HDR illumination.} With a measured HDR environment (a meadow map), the single
  Gaussian and then the full cloud match Mitsuba, confirming the environment importance sampling under
  realistic, high-dynamic-range lighting.
  \item \textbf{Anisotropic phase function.} Henyey--Greenstein scattering with $g \ne 0$ initially
  disagreed with the reference; the cause was a sign error in the phase evaluation, after which forward-
  and back-scattering match Mitsuba.
  \item \textbf{Multiple importance sampling.} Combining phase and environment sampling under the
  balance heuristic exposed a second sign error (in the phase PDF used for the MIS weight); once
  corrected, the MIS estimator matches the reference and reduces variance as expected.
  \item \textbf{Coloured albedo.} Per-channel RGB albedo reproduces the correct tint; a small
  channel-dependent residual remains and is documented as a known limitation (\Cref{ch:conclusion}).
  \item \textbf{Path-control robustness.} Sweeps over maximum depth, Russian-roulette depth, and related
  controls leave the converged result unbiased, confirming these knobs trade noise for time without
  perturbing the mean.
\end{itemize}

\section{The combined showcase}
\label{sec:money-shot}

The features are finally exercised together---the full cloud, under the measured HDR environment, with
forward-scattering Henyey--Greenstein ($g = 0.85$) and MIS direct lighting. Against Mitsuba-analog the
renderer matches the converged image and, at equal quality, reaches it in less time while remaining
free of the fireflies that the reference exhibits on this scene (quantified in \Cref{ch:results}). This
combined result is the correctness foundation on which the performance study of \Cref{ch:optimization}
builds: every optimisation there is required to preserve this image, and is reverted if it does not
(\Cref{fig:showcase}).
